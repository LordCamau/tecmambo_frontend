NIO and Geely are moving battery swapping closer to becoming shared infrastructure in China. The companies have entered a strategic partnership covering battery swapping, charging networks, technology and operating standards.

Under the transaction, Geely will contribute 100% of Yiyi Power and RMB 640 million in cash to NIO Power. After completion, Geely will hold 30% of NIO Power, while NIO China retains a controlling interest. NIO will also take a 10% stake in Geely's Haohan Energy charging business.

[IMAGE 1: NIO battery swapping station]
[CAPTION: NIO's battery-swapping network is at the center of the new partnership.]

## Why battery swapping matters

Fast charging has improved dramatically, but charging still requires the vehicle to remain connected for a period of time. Battery swapping approaches the problem differently. A compatible vehicle can enter a station, remove its depleted battery and receive a charged pack.

That is particularly attractive for commercial fleets, where downtime directly affects revenue. A taxi, delivery vehicle or fleet car can spend less time waiting for energy.

## The problem is compatibility

Battery swapping works best when vehicles share physical, electrical and software standards. If every manufacturer uses a different battery format, stations become isolated assets.

[IMAGE 2: Battery swap station sequence]
[CAPTION: Battery swapping depends on compatible vehicle and battery standards.]

The NIO and Geely agreement directly addresses that problem by planning unified swapping technologies and standards for consumer vehicles. Geely also intends to develop compatible consumer models, while NIO Power will provide swapping services.

## The commercial fleet angle

Yiyi Power focuses on commercial mobility. Its integration into NIO Power gives the combined business a stronger position in a segment where charging speed has direct economic value.

Commercial fleets operate for long hours and can accumulate high mileage. A charging solution that reduces downtime can therefore be worth more to a fleet operator than the same solution is to a private driver.

## NIO and Geely are linking conventional charging too

The partnership is not limited to battery swapping. NIO will acquire a 10% stake in Haohan Energy, while the companies plan to connect their charging infrastructure.

[IMAGE 3: NIO and Geely charging network]
[CAPTION: The alliance also connects the companies' charging infrastructure.]

That matters because EV drivers need multiple energy options. A private owner may charge overnight. A highway driver may need fast charging. A commercial fleet may prefer swapping. A broader network can support all three.

## What this could mean for consumers

If more vehicle models become compatible with the same swap network, consumers gain flexibility. The station becomes more valuable because it can serve more vehicles, while manufacturers can avoid duplicating every infrastructure investment.

The agreement is not yet an industry-wide standard. Regulatory approvals, technical work and compatible vehicle launches still have to follow.

## The economics remain difficult

Battery swapping requires stations, land, machinery and inventories of charged batteries. Operators must predict demand carefully. An underused station ties up capital, while an overloaded station can run short of available batteries.

China is an unusually strong testing ground because of its large EV market and dense urban corridors. Whether the model translates easily to other markets remains an open question.

## The tecMAMBO take

The most important part of the NIO and Geely deal is not the ownership percentages. It is the attempt to reduce fragmentation. Battery swapping becomes much more useful when several vehicle manufacturers can share infrastructure and standards.

If the companies deliver compatible vehicles and a shared network, the partnership could become a template for infrastructure cooperation in a market where automakers usually compete aggressively.

## China is the biggest driver

China remains the world's largest industrial robotics market. The IFR says Chinese factories installed 354,000 robots in 2025, up 20% year over year. China accounted for 59% of global industrial robot deployments.

Domestic Chinese robot suppliers also strengthened their position. The IFR says they reached a 55% share of China's domestic market in 2025.

[IMAGE 2: Automated Chinese manufacturing facility]
[CAPTION: China accounted for 59% of global industrial robot deployments in 2025.]

## These are not mostly humanoid robots

The five-million figure refers primarily to industrial machines such as robotic arms, welding systems, assembly equipment and material-handling robots. It should not be confused with the much newer humanoid robotics industry.

That distinction matters because industrial robotics is already a mature commercial technology. Humanoid robots remain a developing category.

## Why factories keep buying robots

Manufacturers use robots for repeatability, quality, safety and productivity. Robots can perform repetitive operations for long periods without fatigue and can work in environments that may be difficult for people.

Modern systems are also becoming more flexible. Machine vision can help a robot identify objects, inspect components and adapt movements.

[IMAGE 3: Robotic arm with machine vision]
[CAPTION: Machine vision is making industrial automation more adaptable.]

## AI is changing automation

The important shift is not that industrial robots suddenly became intelligent. It is that AI and computer vision are reducing some of the programming and perception work that previously made automation expensive.

Low-code interfaces can also reduce the expertise needed to deploy some systems. This makes automation more accessible to smaller manufacturers.

## What five million robots means for jobs

Automation can remove some tasks while creating demand for engineers, technicians, maintenance specialists and software developers. It can also make factories more competitive and potentially preserve production that might otherwise move elsewhere.

The effect therefore depends on the industry and the tasks being automated.

[IMAGE 4: Human technician beside industrial robot]
[CAPTION: Factory automation is changing jobs as well as equipment.]

## The next phase

The next robotics competition is likely to be about deployment speed, flexibility and software. Manufacturers want machines that can be installed quickly, programmed easily and adapted when products change.

Digital twins, simulation, machine vision and AI-based control are likely to become more important.

## The tecMAMBO take

Five million operational industrial robots is less a futuristic headline than evidence that automation has already become manufacturing infrastructure. China is driving a large share of the growth, but the wider trend is global.

The next advantage may not come from owning the most robots. It may come from making each machine easier to deploy and more useful.

Reducing mechanical components can reduce weight, friction and potential failure points, although direct-drive systems also place demanding requirements on motors, control electronics and thermal management.

A locally assembled vehicle can create economic activity beyond the factory itself. Suppliers may provide steel, plastics, electronics, batteries, software, logistics and maintenance. Training and technical jobs can also grow around the industry.

There is a legitimate trade-off. Fully built imports can reach the market quickly and may be cheaper. Restricting incentives too aggressively could raise vehicle prices and slow EV adoption.

## Kenya could build around more than cars

Kenya's e-mobility opportunity includes motorcycles, buses, three-wheelers and passenger cars. ## The policy needs predictability

Manufacturing investment is long term. Companies need stable rules on taxes, standards, imports and incentives before they commit to factories and tooling. Sudden changes can make investors delay decisions.

That makes policy consistency almost as important as the size of the incentive.

KAM is raising a real industrial policy question. Cheap imports can accelerate EV adoption, while local assembly can create jobs and domestic capability. The challenge is designing incentives that do both where possible.

The strongest version of the policy would make local production commercially competitive rather than simply protected. That means infrastructure, skills, financing, standards and supply chains must improve alongside tax incentives.

## Why an AI policy matters

An AI system can influence access to credit, employment, healthcare or public services. It can also process large quantities of personal information.

## Risk-based regulation

Not every AI application presents the same risk. A creative writing assistant is different from a system used to evaluate loan applications or assist medical decisions.

A risk-based approach can therefore apply stronger controls to high-impact systems while keeping lighter requirements for lower-risk applications.

Potential safeguards include testing, documentation, human oversight, auditability and data controls.

Kenya's challenge is to protect citizens while preserving room for local research and innovation.
