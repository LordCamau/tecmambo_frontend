# An Anthropic researcher just resigned in public, and the industry's reaction says more than his warning did

**Quick answer:** Jacob Coxon, a 27 year old pretraining researcher who worked at both OpenAI and Anthropic over three years, resigned from Anthropic on September 9, 2026, and posted a seven part message on X warning that frontier AI labs are racing toward self improving superintelligence without adequate safeguards. He gave up unvested equity to speak publicly. Anthropic's own alignment lead, Evan Hubinger, responded that Coxon is correct that people inside Anthropic take the risk seriously. Coxon has also said he personally has not seen Anthropic compromise safety to beat competitors. Both things are true at once, and that tension is the actual story.

On the evening of September 9, Jacob Coxon posted on X, under his longstanding handle @hilbertspaess, that he had resigned from Anthropic. His post opened plainly: he had spent three years doing pretraining research, first at OpenAI, then at Anthropic, and he did not believe either company was acting responsibly.

The post spread fast, reportedly reaching tens of millions of views within a day, amplified by a Wall Street Journal interview published the same week. Coxon gave up equity that had not yet vested in order to speak on the record rather than staying quiet until his position was secure.

## What Coxon actually said

Coxon's argument is not that Anthropic's safety work is fabricated. It is that competitive pressure between AI labs is making that safety work insufficient, regardless of how genuine it is.

In his interview with Axios, Coxon put it directly: being under pressure to race forces labs to cut corners or skip steps in oversight. He also offered a more complicated observation that got less attention than his headline warning, that some of the industry's caution is itself distorted by what he called excessive paranoia about OpenAI and about China, paranoia that can end up justifying pushing capability forward faster rather than slower.

Coxon's central technical concern is recursive self improvement, the idea that once AI systems can meaningfully improve their own training and architecture, progress could compound faster than human researchers can monitor or intervene in it. He has called for coordination among labs and a temporary pause on efforts specifically aimed at improving model capability, as distinct from other AI research.

## What Anthropic's own people said back

This is the part worth being precise about, because it is easy to read as either a cover up or a confirmation, and it is neither cleanly.

Evan Hubinger, who leads alignment work at Anthropic, responded publicly rather than through a spokesperson statement, saying Coxon is correct that people inside the company earnestly believe advanced AI could pose a severe risk to humanity, and that he personally would put meaningful odds on a catastrophic outcome this decade. That is a striking thing for an alignment lead at a major AI lab to say on the record, and it happened in response to Coxon, not as a private admission that later leaked.

Coxon has also said, distinctly from his broader warning, that he personally has not seen Anthropic compromise its safety commitments to outlast competitors. That detail is often dropped from summaries of his resignation, but it matters: his argument is about systemic and competitive risk across the industry, not a specific allegation that Anthropic broke a promise internally.

Reporting following the resignation also noted that Anthropic is facing separate questions about its engagement with the UK's AI Security Institute, the government body that evaluates advanced AI systems. That is a live, developing thread and not yet a settled part of the Coxon story itself.

## Why this is different from earlier high profile AI safety exits

Coxon's departure has been compared to earlier resignations at OpenAI, including Jan Leike's 2024 departure, where Leike directly alleged that safety work had lost out to product priorities internally. Coxon's framing is different. He is not alleging that either company broke a specific internal commitment. He is arguing that the structure of competition between labs, each individually acting in reasonably good faith on safety, can still produce a bad collective outcome. That is a more structural claim, and a harder one to fact check against any single company's internal conduct.

## What remains genuinely unverified

Coxon's most viral line, that AI could kill everyone by the end of the decade, is a prediction, not a documented event. It reflects real, stated concern from people close to frontier model development, including Hubinger's own public odds. It is not a confirmed technical finding, and treating it as one in either direction, dismissing it as hype or repeating it as settled fact, would misrepresent what has actually been established here.

What is established: a credentialed researcher who worked inside two leading labs resigned publicly, gave up compensation to do so, and a senior safety researcher at the company he left corroborated the sincerity of his concern rather than disputing it. What remains open is everything about timelines, likelihood, and what specific policy response would actually address the risk Coxon describes.

## The tecMAMBO take

"Xiaomi copied Apple" is a satisfying headline and a misleading one. What actually happened is closer to convergent design under a shared set of rumors: Apple's leak trail had become detailed and consistent enough by late August that any manufacturer designing toward the same passport style category was always going to land somewhere visually adjacent to what eventually shipped. Xiaomi leaned into that resemblance rather than away from it, consistent with a strategy the company has run for years, and got a device to market first as a result.

That is a legitimate strategic and design story. It is not evidence of stolen schematics or compromised supply chains, and treating a visual family resemblance between two foldables, launched two days apart, built on fundamentally different hinge architectures and frame materials, as proof of industrial espionage would be a serious, unsupported accusation against a real company. The more interesting and better supported story is the one about timing and design philosophy, not theft.

# Apple showed up eight years late to foldables. Here's what it actually got right, and what it didn't

**Quick answer:** This is analysis, not neutral reporting. Apple's iPhone Duo arrives eight years after Samsung's original Galaxy Fold, and it improves meaningfully on crease visibility and software continuity compared to older foldables, but it does not eliminate the crease entirely, and Samsung's Z Fold8 has had years of iteration to refine its own software and hinge. Whether the Duo is actually better depends heavily on what you value, not on a clean technical win for either side.

Apple waited eight years after Samsung shipped the original Galaxy Fold to enter the foldable category. That patience is a real part of Apple's playbook, let competitors work through early hardware problems in public, then arrive with a more resolved product. The iPhone Duo is a genuine test of whether that patience paid off against a Samsung foldable that has now had multiple generations to mature.

## Where the Duo is a real improvement

Apple's inner display uses a multi layer laminated construction specifically engineered to reduce the visible crease that has defined folding phones since the category began. It is a meaningful engineering improvement over early folding displays, and reviewers who have gone hands on describe it as smoother and less visually distracting than most rivals.

It is worth being precise about the claim, though: Apple's own materials describe this as crease minimizing, not crease free. The fold line remains detectable at certain viewing angles and under direct light, even if it is far less prominent than on Samsung's original Fold generations. Marketing language and casual coverage have rounded this up to "zero crease" in places, and that overstates what Apple actually delivered.

On software, Apple built iOS specifically for the dual screen form factor rather than adapting its existing phone interface, with apps reshaping automatically across the fold and a navigation layout redesigned around the wider aspect ratio. That is a genuine advantage over the early years of Android foldable software, which leaned on developers manually optimizing for unusual aspect ratios with inconsistent results.

## Where Samsung still has the edge

Samsung's Z Fold8 has years of real world iteration behind its hinge and software that the Duo, as a first generation device, has not had the chance to accumulate. Multi window multitasking on Android's foldable interface has matured across several hardware generations, and Samsung's One UI has had time to work out rough edges that Apple's dual screen iOS has not yet been tested against at scale.

Samsung also isn't standing still on hardware. The Z Fold8 remains a live, iterating product line rather than a fixed target, which means direct comparisons made today will look different a year from now as both companies respond to each other.

## The proportions argument, and why it's the most interesting difference

The most substantive design difference between the two phones isn't the crease, it's the aspect ratio when closed. Apple's iPhone Duo, at 84.1mm wide and 164.6mm tall when open, targets something closer to standard smartphone proportions in its closed state than Samsung's taller, narrower Fold line historically has. Whether that reads as an advantage depends entirely on what you're used to: it makes the Duo feel less like a dedicated tablet-in-your-pocket and more like a normal phone that happens to open, which is a deliberate design choice, not an objectively correct one.

## The tecMAMBO take

Samsung pioneered the commercial foldable and has spent years working through the hardware and software problems that come with the category. Apple entered late with a first generation device that solves some of those problems convincingly, the crease reduction and the software continuity are real, and gets others only partway there, since first generation hardware rarely matches the polish of a product on its fourth or fifth iteration.

Calling this a clean win for either side oversimplifies it. The more accurate read is that Apple's first foldable is a genuinely strong debut that benefits from watching Samsung's mistakes, not a device that definitively surpasses a rival with years more field experience. Buyers choosing between them are choosing between ecosystems and priorities as much as specs, and that was true before either phone existed.

# Apple raised iPhone prices $100, and absorbed a bigger hike itself to keep it that low

**Quick answer:** Apple raised the starting price of the iPhone 18 Pro to $1,199 and the iPhone 18 Pro Max to $1,299, both $100 more than their iPhone 17 predecessors, and simultaneously discontinued the iPhone 17 Pro and Pro Max. Apple also raised prices $100 on the iPhone 16, iPhone 17, iPhone 17e, and iPhone Air, which it kept in its lineup. The increases are tied to a global spike in memory component costs. Analysts at TrendForce had modeled a $150 to $200 increase as necessary to offset those costs, meaning Apple's actual $100 hike came out of its own profit margin rather than being fully passed to buyers.

Global memory prices spiked hard in 2026, driven largely by AI infrastructure demand consuming wafer capacity that would otherwise go toward consumer grade RAM. Apple, which had already raised prices on its Mac, iPad, and home product lines back in June because of the same shortage, extended that pressure to iPhones for the first time this cycle.

## What actually changed

The iPhone 18 Pro now starts at $1,199, and the iPhone 18 Pro Max at $1,299, each $100 above their iPhone 17 Pro equivalents, increases of roughly 9 and 8 percent respectively. Apple also raised prices on models it kept selling: the iPhone 16 to $799, along with $100 increases on the iPhone 17, iPhone 17e, and iPhone Air.

Alongside the price changes, Apple discontinued the iPhone 17 Pro and iPhone 17 Pro Max, pulling both from its online store the same day. That retirement itself is standard practice, Apple typically drops the outgoing Pro generation as soon as its successor launches, and it did the same to the iPhone 16 Pro line in 2025 and the iPhone 15 Pro line in 2024. What's unusual this year is that Apple did not release a standard iPhone 18 alongside the Pro models at all; that model has been pushed to a spring 2027 window alongside a second generation iPhone Air, meaning the current lineup has a wider than usual gap between its most and least expensive current models.

Higher storage configurations saw steeper increases. The 18 Pro Max's new 2TB option costs $500 more than its 1TB tier, and 1TB configurations across the Pro line cost $300 more than their predecessors, both disproportionate to the base model's $100 bump.

## The part that's easy to miss: Apple ate some of this cost itself

This is the detail that changes how the story should be read. Research firm TrendForce had modeled that rising memory costs alone, with 256GB configuration memory pricing running close to 400% higher year over year, justified a price increase in the $150 to $200 range. Some analysts had floated figures as high as $300 for the most memory intensive configurations.

Apple's actual increase, $100 across the Pro lineup, is smaller than what the underlying component math would suggest was necessary. That gap did not disappear; it came out of Apple's own gross margin rather than being passed fully to buyers. Then Apple CEO Tim Cook had signaled this shift was coming as early as June, telling an interviewer that price increases had become unavoidable after the company had spent months trying to shield customers from rising component costs.

## Why this matters beyond the sticker price

This is Apple's second price adjustment tied to memory costs in 2026, following the June increases across Mac, iPad, and home products. Reading the two together suggests this isn't a one time iPhone specific decision but a sustained response to a genuinely tighter global memory market, one that is being driven in significant part by AI data center demand competing directly with consumer electronics for the same wafer capacity.

That dynamic is unlikely to resolve quickly. If AI infrastructure buildout continues consuming high bandwidth memory production capacity at current rates, further consumer hardware price pressure across the industry, not just at Apple, is a reasonable expectation heading into 2027, not a one off correction.

## iPhone 18 Pro and iPhone 18 Pro Max

## The tecMAMBO take

The headline product was always going to be the foldable, and it delivered a genuinely new Apple product category for the first time since the Apple Watch. But the more consequential story from this keynote might be the price increases sitting quietly next to it. Apple absorbing part of a real memory cost spike into its own margin rather than passing all of it to buyers is a deliberate signal about how the company wants to be seen navigating a supply constrained year, and it's a story that will matter more over the next several quarters than any single product announcement does.

It's also worth noting what Apple chose not to change. Watch Ultra 4 keeping an identical case to its predecessor, and Series 12 shipping without the rumored Touch ID or blood-glucose sensor, suggests a company being more conservative with its wearables lineup this cycle than the rumor mill expected, putting its actual engineering effort into the chip, the AI features, and the foldable instead.
